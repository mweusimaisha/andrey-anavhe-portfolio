Background

What PULSE Does

PULSE is a Raleigh-Durham event series celebrating African culture through food, music, and community. Their events — Bolé & Party, Brunch & Banter, Afro-Karaoke & Game Night — pull in crowds of 150–300+ people and have built a real following in the area.

I came on as Event Coordinator in December 2023. The events were already good. My job was to make sure the behind-the-scenes operation matched the quality of what guests were experiencing.

The Situation

What Needed to Change

The Problem

As events got bigger and more frequent, the lack of formal production processes started showing. Vendors showed up without clear briefings. Staff weren't sure who owned what on the day. Budget tracking was loose. Small things kept falling through the cracks.

What I Did

Built production docs from scratch — run-of-show templates, vendor trackers, pre-event timelines, and a post-event closeout report. Set up a communication structure for event day: radio channels by department, clear role assignments before anyone stepped on site.

What Changed

Zero cancellations over two years. Budget variance stayed under 5% across events. The team stopped spending energy on day-of chaos and could actually focus on the guest experience. Vendors started showing up prepared because they knew exactly what to expect.

The Work

Specifically What I Built

  • Run-of-show documents for every event — load-in through closeout, phase by phase, with named owners and contingency notes for the stuff that actually goes wrong (vendor late, AV failure, crowd overages).
  • A vendor tracker that kept contracts, deposit status, final payments, and day-of contacts in one place. Eliminated the "who do I call?" scramble that was eating time on event days.
  • Budget management on events from $5K to $40K — tracked actuals against line items in real time, negotiated vendor costs without cutting quality, and built in a contingency buffer on every event.
  • On-site team structure for 10–15 staff and contractors — pre-event briefings, radio channel assignments by department, real-time adjustments when things shifted.
  • A post-event closeout report sent to the founder within 48 hours: attendance, actual vs. budget spend, what worked, what needs fixing before the next one.
What I Took Away

Honest Reflections

📋
A good run-of-show makes your team confident. When people know exactly what they're responsible for and when, they stop looking over their shoulder and start actually doing the job. That was the biggest shift I noticed.
💰
Vendor relationships matter more than vendor contracts. The savings I found weren't from squeezing rates — they came from vendors who trusted us and gave us flexibility because we'd treated them well on previous events.
🎯
I'd push for earlier vendor confirmations next time. Three days out is too late to be chasing final confirmations. I've since moved that window to a week out as a hard rule.

Background

The Setup at WinzTV

WinzTV creates sports and culture content for African and Afro-diaspora audiences. I led a team of six — editors, designers, a host, and a writer — all working remotely on different schedules.

When I joined, the team was capable but disorganized. Good content was getting made, but it was taking longer than it should and costing more revision rounds than it needed to. The process from brief to published piece had a lot of unnecessary back-and-forth built into it.

The Situation

What Was Actually Happening

The Problem

Briefs were given verbally or in a quick Slack message. Feedback came through DMs, voice notes, and emails — often vague. The team would go back and forth 3–4 times on a single piece because nobody was clear on what "approved" looked like until it was overdue.

What I Did

Introduced a written content brief for every piece, set up Monday.com sprint boards for the team, ran a 30-minute weekly review, and built a simple revision protocol — written feedback only, batched, with timestamps on video notes.

What Changed

Revision cycles dropped by around 40%. We went from producing content reactively to running on a consistent weekly schedule. The team spent less time on admin chaos and more time on actual production.

The Work

What I Put in Place

  • A content brief template covering concept, audience, tone, deliverables with specs, script structure, and a revision protocol — so every piece started with the same foundation and nobody had to guess what was expected.
  • Monday.com boards organized by content piece — tasks assigned per team member, due dates, and status columns. When something was stuck, it showed up before it became a missed deadline.
  • Weekly sprint reviews, Mondays, 30 minutes. What's in progress, what's blocked, what's publishing this week. Enough structure to stay aligned without over-meeting a small creative team.
  • A revision protocol that required all feedback in writing, in the task thread, batched before submission. That one change — more than anything else — cut the longest revision cycles.
  • Reference folders in Monday.com linking to past pieces that got the tone, pacing, and style right — so editors and designers had something concrete to aim at on new briefs.
What I Took Away

Honest Reflections

📝
Most revision problems start at the brief stage. Almost every piece that went 3+ rounds had a brief that skipped something — audience, tone, or what "done" actually looked like. Once I tightened briefs, the revisions followed.
🔁
The revision protocol was the highest-leverage change. Forcing written, timestamped, batched feedback sounds like overhead but it made everyone — including me — give better notes. Vague feedback is a habit, not a personality.
🌍
Cultural context belongs in the brief. For content aimed at Afro-diaspora audiences specifically, tone and references had to be explicit — not assumed. When I left that out, the content felt off even if the execution was technically fine.

Background

What I Walked Into

When I joined Budget/Avis Knightdale as Operations Manager, I was managing a 218-vehicle fleet with a team of 14. The day-to-day wasn't broken — vehicles were going out, coming back, getting prepped. But there was no reliable way for leadership to see what was actually happening on the floor at any given time.

The logs were manual and inconsistent. Problems were usually discovered after the fact — after a customer waited too long, or after a maintenance issue turned into something bigger. The data existed in theory; in practice it wasn't usable.

The Situation

The Actual Problem

The Problem

31 reporting gaps per month at baseline. Manual vehicle logs were being skipped, done late, or filled out inconsistently. Nobody was trying to create problems — the process just made it easy to let things slip, and there was no system catching it.

What I Did

Audited the existing process first — mapped every step of the vehicle lifecycle, found exactly where logs were getting missed. Then designed a digital tracking workflow to replace the manual system and trained all 14 staff before cutting over.

What Changed

Reporting gaps dropped from 31/month to 6 within 90 days. Accuracy hit 98%. Customer wait time fell from 18 minutes to 7. The process has been running as the location standard since January 2026.

The Work

How It Came Together

  • Spent the first three weeks auditing — not building. Mapped the full vehicle lifecycle from return to re-fleet and identified 6 specific handoff points where logs were consistently missed. That's what the solution targeted.
  • Redesigned the logging process digitally, cutting the steps required to update a vehicle's status from 4 down to 1. The biggest driver of non-compliance was friction, not attitude — so I removed the friction.
  • Ran a 2-week parallel period where the team used both systems before fully switching over. That made the training stick and gave people a chance to catch issues before the old process was gone.
  • Built a weekly summary report that went to leadership automatically — fleet status, utilization, maintenance queue, staffing coverage. No manual assembly required on either end.
  • Added a simple daily compliance check: if the morning log wasn't complete by 9 AM, the floor lead got a prompt. Small accountability loop that turned consistency into a habit within a few weeks.
What I Took Away

Honest Reflections

🔍
Auditing first saved a lot of time. I almost skipped it. The temptation was to just roll out a new system and see what happened. Finding the 6 specific failure points meant the solution was targeted instead of general.
🤝
Getting the team on board was harder than building the tracker. A few people were skeptical — they'd seen "new systems" come and go. Showing early wins fast, and making their jobs easier rather than more complicated, was what actually moved them.
📊
Visibility changes how people work. Once the floor team knew that their output was visible in real time, accountability shifted — not because anyone was being monitored, but because the work felt like it mattered and connected to something bigger.
